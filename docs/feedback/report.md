# Phase 24 — Human-in-the-loop feedback

Generated by `scripts/feedback_sim.py`. Raw results: [`simulation.json`](simulation.json) ·
figure: [`loop.png`](loop.png).

A deployed model gets worse in ways training never sees, and the only new information after
launch comes from the people reading its output. This phase builds that channel — a review
UI, a local database, and a policy that turns ratings into per-label decision thresholds —
and then, because untested infrastructure is a hypothesis rather than a system, measures
whether the loop actually helps.

**It does not, in its obvious form.** Feedback collected the natural way makes the model
worse. The measurement of *why*, and what fixes it, is the substance of this phase.

---

## 1. What was built

**Review UI** (`app/frontend/feedback_panel.py`) — under every report, one row per finding
with **correct / incorrect / uncertain**, a box for findings APEX *missed*, a reviewer ID
and a comment. Submission is blocked until every visible finding is rated: a partially
completed review is worse than none, because unrated findings are indistinguishable from
silently accepted ones and pooling the two inflates precision.

**Store** (`src/feedback/store.py`) — plain SQLite. Alongside the verdict it records three
things that are worthless to reconstruct later: the **confidence** at the moment of review
("incorrect at 0.55" and "incorrect at 0.98" are different evidence), the **threshold in
force**, and whether the finding was an **exploratory probe** shown deliberately below
threshold. Missed findings are a separate table, not a rating — a false negative is not a
verdict on something APEX said.

**Policy** (`src/feedback/policy.py`) — per-label thresholds, applied at inference through
`analyze_signal(..., thresholds=...)`. Default behaviour is byte-identical to every earlier
phase.

## 2. The problem: a reviewer can only rate what they were shown

Every rating is drawn from *above* the current threshold. The only error visible up there
is a false positive. So the data can always justify raising a threshold and can never
justify lowering one — and a loop trained on it moves in one direction forever. Precision
climbs, the dashboard improves every week, and recall quietly collapses.

This is verification bias, and no amount of statistical care fixes it, because the data
genuinely does not contain the answer. Two things put the information back:

- **Reviewers report what was missed** — the only direct evidence about recall.
- **Exploration** — a small fraction of *sub-threshold* findings are surfaced anyway,
  labelled as probes, so the region below the threshold gets sampled at all.

## 3. Does the loop help? Four arms, measured

Feedback streams from the **validation** fold (2,183 records); every score is on the
**test** fold (2,198). The reviewer is simulated from PTB-XL labels. Thresholds start at
the shipped global 0.5.

| Arm | Threshold moves | Δ macro-P | Δ macro-R | Δ macro-F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** no feedback | — | — | — | — (F1 0.2136) |
| **B** ratings only | 9 up, **0 down** | +0.007 | −0.007 | **−0.0045** (−2.1%) |
| **C** + missed findings reported | 10 up, **0 down** | +0.007 | −0.007 | **−0.0045** (−2.1%) |
| **D** + exploration | **1 up, 18 down** | −0.033 | **+0.049** | **+0.0221** (+10.3%) |
| **E** D, fallible reviewer | 1 up, 19 down | −0.031 | +0.046 | **+0.0199** (+9.3%) |

**The ratchet is real and it is total.** Arms B and C moved 19 thresholds between them and
*not one of them went down*. Feedback collected the obvious way does not merely fail to
help — it costs 2.1% of macro-F1, and it would keep costing more the longer it ran.

**Letting reviewers report missed findings does not fix it.** This is the result worth
pausing on, because it is the intuitive remedy. Arm C collected **1,647 reported misses**
and still moved 10 thresholds up and zero down — performance identical to arm B to four
decimal places. Knowing a true positive sits below the threshold does not tell you how many
*false* positives sit beside it, so it still cannot justify lowering anything. Recall
evidence and precision evidence are different things, and only one of them was collected.

**Exploration flips the direction.** 504 probes — under 10% of ratings — turn 9-up/0-down
into 1-up/18-down and buy **+0.049 macro recall for −0.033 precision**, a net **+10.3%**
macro-F1. That is the entire difference between a loop that decays and a loop that learns.

**It survives a fallible reviewer.** Arm E's clinician is wrong 10% of the time, abstains
near the decision boundary, and reports only about a third of misses. It keeps 90% of the
gain (+9.3% vs +10.3%). The loop degrades gracefully rather than inverting, which is what
the shrinkage and the move caps are for.

### A bug this experiment caught

The first policy had a fallback: when no threshold reached the target precision, raise by
one capped step. It seemed conservative. It was not — **only 21 of 71 labels can reach
precision 0.70 at any threshold on this model**, so for the other 50 the rule fired on
every update and ratcheted them upward forever, buying nothing and destroying recall. It
cost **3.4 points of macro-F1** before removal.

A threshold trades recall for precision along the model's existing ROC curve; it cannot
manufacture precision the curve does not contain. When the target is unreachable, the
finding is that *the model* needs work on that label, and the policy now says so
(`target_unreachable`) and leaves the threshold alone. A test asserts the fallback stays
deleted.

## 4. How this would be used over time

The loop is one instrument, not a strategy. In deployment order:

**Weeks 0–4 — collect, change nothing.** Thresholds stay frozen while the database fills
and inter-rater agreement is measured on deliberately double-assigned reports. If two
clinicians disagree with each other as often as they disagree with the model, the feedback
is measuring reviewers and no threshold should move at all.

**Month 2 onward — re-threshold, slowly.** Per-label updates with the guardrails already in
the policy: ≥20 informative ratings, a Beta lower bound rather than a point estimate, ≤0.10
movement per update. Roughly a fifth of labels will have enough evidence to move; the rest
correctly do nothing. Every change ships as a versioned `ThresholdSet` and is reversible.

**Continuously — spend exploration where recall is leaking.** Probe rate rises for labels
with reported misses and sits at a 5% floor elsewhere. This is a real cost — a clinician's
attention on something the model did not believe — so it is budgeted, capped at 25%, and
never applied below 0.20 confidence.

**Quarterly — retrain, using what thresholds cannot fix.** The `target_unreachable` list is
the phase's most useful by-product: labels where no operating point is acceptable, ranked
by review volume. That is a data-collection and modelling backlog derived from deployment
rather than guessed at. Findings rated `incorrect` at high confidence are the natural hard-
negative pool; reported misses are the hard positives.

**Always — audit the ratchet.** The single number to watch is the up/down ratio of threshold
moves. Arms B and C show what a compromised loop looks like: every move in one direction.
If that appears in production, exploration has stopped working and the loop should be
paused, not tuned.

## 5. Limitations

- **The reviewer is simulated.** PTB-XL labels stand in for a clinician; arm E models error
  and abstention but no real feedback has been collected. The infrastructure is exercised;
  the clinical behaviour it would meet is not.
- **Improvement is measured against PTB-XL's annotations**, which Phase 22 showed differ
  72% of the time at zero elapsed time. "Better" here means better agreement with a noisy
  reference.
- **Thresholds only.** The loop re-tunes decisions, it does not update weights. Online
  gradient updates on clinician feedback would be a far larger change with a far worse
  failure mode, and are deliberately out of scope.
- **Recall is estimated from an incomplete sample.** True positives below the 0.20
  exploration floor are invisible, so the F1 estimate is biased slightly high and chosen
  thresholds slightly conservative. Calibrating this properly needs periodic exhaustive
  review of a random sample — standard audit-sampling practice, and not implemented here.
- **Single-reviewer verdicts are taken at face value** once agreement is acceptable; no
  reviewer-reliability weighting is modelled.
- **2,183 reviewed reports is a lot of clinician time.** At a realistic review rate this is
  months of work, and the +10.3% is the ceiling that volume buys, not a monthly rate.
